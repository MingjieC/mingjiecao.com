Mingjie Cao

Ph.D. Student in Management (Accounting)
Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University

My research lies at the intersection of political economy and capital markets. I am interested in how geopolitics and U.S. politics, from armed conflicts to political donations, shape what firms earn and disclose, and how market participants assess them. These questions bring together my long-standing curiosity about world affairs and my training in economics.

Before joining McGill as a Ph.D. student in accounting, I earned an M.A. in Economics from the University of Toronto and an Honours B.Sc. in Economics from the Université de Montréal. Prior to university, I completed all of my schooling in French in Montréal. I am also the founding president of the Montreal Accounting PhD Club, which organizes social events and activities for accounting doctoral students from the city’s four business schools.

Working Papers

Do Analysts Understand Geopolitics? Evidence from Foreign Demand Shocks (sole-authored)

Presented at McGill University, Desautels Faculty of Management, 2026

In my words

To me, geopolitics is one of the hardest things to read. It spans international relations, economics, and history, and its shocks, from armed conflicts to trade wars, hit different countries in different ways.

That led me to ask how well financial analysts handle it. Their job is to translate news into earnings forecasts, yet their expertise lies in industries and companies, not foreign politics. So I wanted to know whether they get a geopolitical shock’s impact on the companies they cover right.

What I find is a kind of half-understanding. Analysts cut their forecasts for the right companies and in the right direction, but at first by only about a third of the eventual drop in earnings. They see who will be hurt, but not how badly.

Abstract

Do sell-side analysts understand what geopolitical shocks do to firms’ earnings? I measure their understanding by how much of a shock’s predictable effect on a firm’s earnings their forecast revisions incorporate, using 39 shocks of economic measures or war to 22 countries between 1995 and 2025. The predictable effect comes from where firms sell: the greater a U.S. firm’s sales exposure to the shocked country, measured from its last 10-K before the shock, the further its earnings fall below analysts’ pre-shock forecasts. Analysts’ revisions incorporate only about a third of this predictable shortfall before the firm’s next earnings announcement, and about two-thirds by their final forecast of the year’s earnings. The under-reaction is not inattention, missed exposure, reluctance to deliver bad news or inexperience. Analysts revise in the right direction, for good news as well as bad, but too little, and their forecasts for exposed firms do not spread apart. They revise less after wars, whose consequences are hardest to gauge, than after economic measures. Analysts understand which firms a geopolitical shock hurts, but they underestimate how much it hurts them.

Measuring Firm-Level Geopolitical Exposure (sole-authored)

Presented at the 12th Annual International Corporate Governance Society (ICGS) Conference, HEC Montréal, 2026 (nominated for the Best Paper Award); McGill University, Desautels Faculty of Management, 2025

In my words

When the U.S.–China trade war erupted, when Russia invaded Ukraine, and when conflict spread in the Middle East, I kept coming back to the same question: which companies will this actually hit?

I noticed that researchers can track when geopolitical risk is high around the world, but not who is exposed to it, and companies rarely say so clearly. So I let the stock market reveal it. If a company’s stock keeps moving whenever geopolitical tensions rise, that company is probably more exposed than most.

That idea became this paper, in which I use stock market reactions to measure each company’s exposure to geopolitics.

Abstract

Existing research has developed important measures of aggregate geopolitical risk, but it remains difficult to assess which individual firms are most exposed when that risk rises during wars, trade disputes, or other geopolitical shocks. I develop GeoX, a market-implied measure of firm-level geopolitical exposure based on firms’ historical excess-return sensitivities to news-based geopolitical shock series over 2015–2023. Firms receive higher GeoX scores when their returns respond more strongly to geopolitical shocks. GeoX identifies economically interpretable exposure patterns, especially among oil, gas, and drilling firms. In out-of-sample validation, high-GeoX firms earn lower excess returns over the 2023–2025 period and experience larger negative cumulative abnormal returns after the October 2023 Middle East shock. The evidence suggests that firm-level geopolitical exposure is measurable, distinct from aggregate geopolitical risk, and informative about cross-sectional return behavior.
